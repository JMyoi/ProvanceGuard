# Provenance Guard

A backend service that a creative-sharing platform can call to assess whether a
piece of **text** was written by a human or generated by AI. It classifies
submitted content with **two independent detection signals**, combines them into
a **calibrated confidence score** (not a binary flag), surfaces a plain-language
**transparency label** to readers, and gives creators an **appeals** path. Every
decision and appeal is captured in a structured **audit log**, and the submission
endpoint is **rate-limited**.

> Design principle throughout: **on a writing platform, wrongly labeling a
> human's work as AI is worse than missing some AI text.** The scoring bands, the
> label copy, and the appeal path all lean cautious because of that asymmetry.

The full pre-implementation spec (signal definitions, scoring math, label copy,
edge cases, AI-tool plan) lives in [planning.md](planning.md).

## Architecture overview — the path a submission takes

A creator sends `POST /submit` with `{ text, creator_id }`. The request first
passes the **rate limiter** (rejected with `429` if over quota). The raw text
then fans out to **two independent detectors**:

1. **Signal 1 — Groq LLM** ([llm_signal.py](llm_signal.py)) returns a 0–1
   probability that the text is AI-generated, plus a one-line rationale.
2. **Signal 2 — Stylometry** ([stylometric_signal.py](stylometric_signal.py))
   returns its own 0–1 score from structural statistics.

Both scores flow into the **confidence scorer**
([scoring.py](scoring.py)), which combines them into a single calibrated
`confidence` and an `attribution` bucket (`likely_ai` / `uncertain` /
`likely_human`). The **label generator** ([labels.py](labels.py)) turns that into
the reader-facing `transparency_label`. A unique `content_id` is minted, the whole
decision is written to the **SQLite audit log** ([audit.py](audit.py)), and the
JSON response returns.

For **appeals**: `POST /appeal` with `{ content_id, creator_reasoning }` looks up
the original decision, flips its `status` to `under_review`, writes an appeal row
linked by `content_id`, and returns a confirmation. `GET /log` surfaces the audit
trail as JSON.

```

{text,             (429 if     │                                        ▼
 creator_id}        over)      └─► Signal 2: Stylometry ─styl_score(0-1)─► Confidence
```

## Detection signals

The pipeline uses **two genuinely independent signals** — one *semantic*, one
*structural*. Their independence is the point: each is blind to what the other
sees, so combining them is more informative than either alone, and their
*disagreement* is itself a useful signal (see scoring below).

### Signal 1 — Groq LLM classifier (semantic / holistic)
- **Measures:** whether the text *reads* as machine-generated — hedged,
  over-balanced, uniformly polished prose; formulaic transitions
  ("It is important to note…", "Furthermore…"); absence of a personal stake or
  idiosyncratic voice.
- **Why I chose it:** humans take positions, wander, and write with a
  recognizable voice; instruction-tuned LLMs default to balanced, tidy, low-risk
  prose. A model reading holistically catches this in a way counting statistics
  can't.
- **Output:** `llm_score` ∈ [0,1] = P(AI-generated), plus a rationale. Model
  `llama-3.3-70b-versatile`, `temperature=0`.
- **What it misses:** can be fooled by lightly human-edited AI text; may over-flag
  formal or non-native-English human writing; non-deterministic and costs an API
  call.

### Signal 2 — Stylometric analyzer (structural / statistical, pure Python)
Computes three measurable statistics and maps them to one 0–1 AI-likelihood
(weights in parentheses):
- **Sentence-length variance / burstiness (0.50):** AI writing is statistically
  *uniform* — similar sentence lengths; human writing is *bursty* — short punchy
  sentences next to long ones. Measured as the coefficient of variation of
  sentence lengths. Weighted highest: it's the most reliable discriminator.
- **Punctuation density (0.30):** casual human text is sparsely punctuated; tidy
  AI prose is moderately, consistently punctuated.
- **Type-token ratio / vocabulary diversity (0.20):** unique words ÷ total words.
  Weighted **lowest** because it's the noisiest — heavily length-dependent, and
  casual human text can look repetitive (see Known limitations).
- **Why I chose it:** it's fully independent of the LLM (no semantics at all), it's
  free and deterministic, and it corroborates or contradicts the LLM's holistic
  read with hard numbers.
- **Output:** `stylometric_score` ∈ [0,1], plus a `reliable` flag.
- **What it misses:** unreliable on **short text** (< 40 words — too little data,
  so `reliable=False`); a repetitive, simple-vocabulary human poem looks
  "AI-uniform"; polished human academic prose scores high.

## Confidence scoring

### What the score means (a design decision first)
`confidence` is **P(AI-generated) ∈ [0,1]**. `0.5` is defined to mean *"we
genuinely cannot tell"* — an honest shrug, not a hidden coin flip. A `0.95` should
feel like a strong claim; a `0.55` should feel like a hedge. The **"uncertain"
band is intentionally wide** so borderline cases resolve to "we're not sure"
rather than an accusation.

### How the two signals combine
```
combined = 0.65 * llm_score + 0.35 * stylometric_score
```
- **LLM weighted higher (0.65):** it reasons about meaning holistically and is the
  more reliable single indicator; stylometry (0.35) is a corroborating structural
  check that's noisier, especially on short text.
- **Disagreement guard:** if `|llm_score − stylometric_score| > 0.40`, the result
  is **forced to `uncertain`** regardless of the average — two blind detectors
  that conflict shouldn't yield a confident verdict.
- **Short text:** if stylometry is `reliable=False` (< 40 words), the scorer leans
  **entirely on the LLM** rather than trusting sparse statistics.

### Thresholds (asymmetric on purpose)
| combined score | attribution     | reader sees          |
|----------------|-----------------|----------------------|
| `>= 0.75`      | `likely_ai`     | Likely AI-generated  |
| `0.45 – 0.75`  | `uncertain`     | Uncertain origin     |
| `< 0.45`       | `likely_human`  | Likely human-written |

The AI threshold (`0.75`) sits higher than the human threshold's mirror would —
**it takes more evidence to call something AI than to leave it alone**, because a
false "AI" accusation is the costly error.

### How I validated it's meaningful
I ran a 4-input calibration harness ([test_calibration.py](test_calibration.py))
spanning clearly-AI, clearly-human, and two borderline cases, printing *both*
signal scores separately so I could see which one drove each verdict. Success
criteria: clear cases land in the right bucket, and borderline cases land in
`uncertain` (or at least well away from a false accusation). Results:

| Input | `llm_score` | `stylometric_score` | **combined** | attribution |
|-------|-------------|---------------------|--------------|-------------|
| Clearly AI (formal, uniform) | 0.90 | 0.59 | **0.79** | `likely_ai` |
| Clearly human (casual review) | 0.10 | 0.29 | **0.17** | `likely_human` |
| Borderline: formal human (economics) | 0.80 | 0.54 | **0.71** | `uncertain` |
| Borderline: lightly edited AI (short) | 0.40–0.60 | 0.67 *(unreliable)* | **~0.40–0.60** | `likely_human` / `uncertain` |

The clear cases separate sharply (0.79 vs 0.17). The formal-human case is held out
of a false accusation (see below). The lightly-edited-AI case is the honest hard
one: the LLM's read of it wanders run-to-run (≈0.4–0.6), and because the text is
short the verdict rests on the LLM alone — so it drifts around the
human/uncertain boundary. That instability *is* the truthful answer: lightly
human-edited AI is close to undetectable, and the system doesn't pretend otherwise.

### Two example submissions with noticeably different confidence

**High-confidence result** — clearly AI-generated text:
> confidence **0.79**, attribution `likely_ai`
> `llm_score=0.90`, `stylometric_score=0.59` — both signals agree it's uniform and formulaic.

**Lower-confidence result** — a genuine but formal human paragraph:
> confidence **0.71**, attribution `uncertain`
> `llm_score=0.80`, `stylometric_score=0.54`.
> This is the design working as intended: the LLM alone would have called this
> `likely_ai` (0.80, a **false positive against a human writer**), but blending in
> the structural signal pulled the combined score to 0.71 — *below* the 0.75 AI
> threshold — so the reader sees an honest "uncertain" instead of an accusation,
> and the creator can appeal.

## Transparency label

Three variants, one per attribution bucket. Plain language for a non-technical
reader; confidence is shown as a rounded percentage. `{pct}` = `round(confidence × 100)`.

| Variant | Exact text displayed |
|---------|----------------------|
| **High-confidence AI** (`likely_ai`) | ⚠️ **Likely AI-generated.** Our analysis suggests this content was probably created with AI assistance (confidence: {pct}%). This is an automated estimate, not a certainty — the creator can appeal if they believe this is wrong. |
| **High-confidence human** (`likely_human`) | ✅ **Likely human-written.** Our analysis found no strong signs of AI generation (confidence: {pct}% that this is AI). This is an automated estimate and not a guarantee of authorship. |
| **Uncertain** (`uncertain`) | ❓ **Uncertain origin.** Our signals disagree or are inconclusive, so we can't reliably say whether this was written by a human or AI (confidence: {pct}% that this is AI). We're showing this openly rather than guessing. The creator can provide context via an appeal. |

Design notes: no variant states a verdict as fact — each says "estimate" and,
for anything other than a clean human result, points to the appeal path. On the
human label the percentage is framed as "% that this is AI" so a low number
(e.g. 12%) reads reassuringly.

Live examples (matching the committed audit-log sample):
- `⚠️ Likely AI-generated. …(confidence: 81%)…`
- `✅ Likely human-written. …(confidence: 12% that this is AI)…`
- `❓ Uncertain origin. …(confidence: 71% that this is AI)…`

## Appeals workflow

- **Who:** the creator of a submission (identified by `content_id`; in a real
  system tied to the authenticated `creator_id`).
- **What they provide:** `content_id` + `creator_reasoning` (free text).
- **What happens on receipt** ([app.py](app.py) → [audit.py](audit.py)):
  1. Look up the original classification by `content_id` (`404` if unknown).
  2. Update that content's `status` → `under_review`.
  3. Write an **appeal row** linked by `content_id`, preserving the contested
     attribution/confidence beside the creator's reasoning and a timestamp.
  4. Return `{ content_id, status:"under_review", message }`.
- **What a reviewer sees** via `GET /log`: the original classification row (both
  signal scores + combined + attribution) sitting next to the appeal row
  (reasoning + timestamp) — the contested decision and the creator's context side
  by side. Automated re-classification is intentionally *not* performed; a human
  makes the call.

```bash
curl -s -X POST http://localhost:5000/appeal \
  -H "Content-Type: application/json" \
  -d '{"content_id": "PASTE-CONTENT-ID", "creator_reasoning": "I wrote this myself; I am a non-native English speaker and write formally."}'
```

## Rate limiting

Applied to `POST /submit` via Flask-Limiter (in-memory storage, keyed by client
IP):

```
10 per minute; 100 per day
```

**Reasoning for these specific numbers:**
- **10 / minute** — a real creator submits their own work occasionally, not in
  bursts. Ten submissions a minute already comfortably covers someone iterating on
  a draft and resubmitting, while a script trying to flood the classifier (and
  burn our paid LLM calls) trips the limit almost immediately.
- **100 / day** — an upper bound on legitimate single-creator volume. Even a very
  active writer posting many pieces stays well under 100/day; a scraper or abuse
  script attempting mass classification does not. It also caps daily Groq API cost
  per client.
- Both limits protect the **cost and availability** of the LLM signal, which is
  the expensive, rate-limited dependency in the system.

**Evidence** — firing 12 rapid requests (limit 10/min):
```
request 1..10 -> HTTP 200
request 11    -> HTTP 429
request 12    -> HTTP 429
```
The `429` body is JSON (a custom handler replaces Flask's default HTML page):
```json
{ "error": "Rate limit exceeded. Please slow down and try again later.", "limit": "10 per 1 minute" }
```

## Known limitations

Detection is not, and cannot be, perfect — the goal is to acknowledge uncertainty
honestly and provide an appeal path. Two specific content types this system will
likely misclassify, tied to properties of its signals:

1. **Repetitive, simple-vocabulary human poetry.** Deliberate repetition and short
   lines drive sentence-length variance *and* type-token ratio *down* — exactly
   the pattern the stylometric signal reads as "AI-uniform." So Signal 2 will push
   such a poem toward AI even though a human wrote it. Mitigation: the LLM often
   disagrees (it recognizes creative voice), and a large enough disagreement trips
   the guard that forces `uncertain` — but a poem that reads flatly to the LLM too
   could still be misjudged. This is a direct consequence of using length/diversity
   statistics as a signal.

2. **Formal writing by non-native English speakers.** Careful, formal, low-variance
   prose from a human learner triggers *both* signals toward AI (uniform structure
   + "reads formulaic" to the LLM). This is the exact scenario the false-positive
   asymmetry is built for: the wide uncertain band and higher AI threshold keep
   many of these in `uncertain` rather than `likely_ai`, and the appeal flow exists
   precisely so the creator can supply context a detector can't see.

A related structural limit: **very short submissions (< 40 words)** don't give the
stylometric signal enough data, so it's flagged unreliable and the verdict rests
on the LLM alone.

## Spec reflection

**One way the spec helped:** deciding the *disagreement guard* and *asymmetric
thresholds* in `planning.md` — before writing code — is what made the
false-positive protection real instead of accidental. When I ran the formal-human
test input, the LLM returned 0.80 (a would-be `likely_ai`), but because the spec
had already committed to blending signals and to a 0.75 AI threshold, the
implementation produced `uncertain` (0.71) with no special-casing. The behavior I
wanted fell out of decisions I'd written down, rather than something I had to
retrofit after seeing a bad result.

**One way the implementation diverged:** `planning.md` said short text should
"down-weight the stylometric signal *and bias toward uncertain*." In code I kept
the down-weighting (stylometry is dropped when unreliable) but **did not** force
`uncertain` — the scorer leans entirely on the LLM instead. I changed this because
the LLM is genuinely reliable at judging short, obviously-casual text (the ramen
review scored 0.10 correctly); forcing everything short into `uncertain` would
have thrown away a correct, confident human verdict and made the label less useful.
Short text is a stylometry problem, not necessarily an overall-uncertainty problem.

## AI usage

I used an AI coding assistant (Claude) throughout, driven by the `planning.md`
spec and architecture diagram. Two specific instances:

1. **Stylometric signal + scoring logic.** I directed the AI to generate the
   stylometric function (burstiness, punctuation density, TTR → one score) and the
   `combine_scores` function *against the exact weights and thresholds in
   `planning.md`* (0.65/0.35, disagreement guard, 0.75/0.45 buckets). It produced a
   reasonable implementation, but I **verified the generated thresholds matched the
   spec** and tuned the stylometric sub-metric ramps (e.g., the sentence-length CV
   mapping) empirically against the four calibration inputs until clearly-AI vs
   clearly-human separated cleanly — the first draft's mappings were plausible but
   not calibrated to real samples.

2. **Groq signal robustness.** I first had the AI use Groq's strict JSON-mode
   (`response_format={"type":"json_object"}`). In testing, the model occasionally
   emitted a stray brace and Groq's validator rejected the whole response with a
   400. I **overrode** that approach: dropped strict JSON mode in favor of a
   defensive parser (try `json.loads`, then extract the first `{…}` block, then
   regex the probability out as a last resort) plus a one-retry loop. This made the
   signal resilient to minor model malformation instead of failing the request. I
   also replaced the default HTML `429` page with a JSON error handler to keep the
   API consistent.
